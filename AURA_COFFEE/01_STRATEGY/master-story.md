# PART ONE — THE INTERROGATION

The fifteen questions, answered from source. Where an answer is not in the source material it is marked.

---

### Why does Aura grow coffee?

Because coffee was already growing there.

Arvind Singh came to 150 acres in the Western Ghats knowing nothing about farming — his own words, and he treats it as the advantage. The land was a working coffee estate. The question was never *what shall we plant*, it was *what is this land actually doing, and what would it do if someone paid attention.*

Coffee is the estate's primary crop and its most demanding student. It reacts to everything — light, soil biology, rainfall timing, the hour a ferment is stopped. Which makes it the perfect readout. If the ground is getting better, the cup says so first.

Coffee is also the part of the system a stranger can hold. Nobody outside the estate will ever taste the soil.

---

### Why regenerative coffee?

Because the alternatives are exhausted, and one of them is a lie.

*Organic* only says what you did not do. *Sustainable* asks what, exactly, is worth sustaining about ground that has been degrading for forty years. Regenerative is the only word that sets a direction: the land should be measurably better at the end of the season than at the start.

It is also the only claim that can be checked. You can audit an absence of chemicals. You can measure a rising earthworm count.

---

### What is wrong with the conventional model?

Conventional coffee runs on an import. Fertility arrives in a sack, gets used by the crop, and the ground is a little poorer for the next round — so next year's sack is bigger. The canopy is cleared to raise yield, which raises heat, which raises water demand and pest pressure, which raises the input bill again.

It works. It works for about as long as the soil lasts.

`[NEEDS SOURCE]` — external figures on soil organic-carbon decline, input-cost escalation, and canopy loss in Indian coffee. Do not publish comparative claims about "most coffee" without a citable source. Everything Aura says about the conventional model should be framed as *what we chose not to do*, which needs no external proof.

**The honest version, which we can publish today:**
Aura does not buy fertility. It makes it, on site, from an animal that eats the same ground the coffee grows in.

---

### Why does soil matter?

Because soil is not dirt with things in it. It is a living body, and it is where the cup is actually decided.

Aura reads the soil as a set of vital signs, not a nutrient statement:
- **Respiration** — the CO₂ the ground gives off as its life works. Read monthly, 0–15 cm. It moves months before anything shows in the canopy.
- **Earthworms** — counted quarterly in a fixed 50 × 50 cm quadrat, sorted into surface-dwellers, topsoil-workers, and deep burrowers.
- **Mycorrhizae** — percent of root length colonised, annually.
- **Aggregate stability, infiltration, organic carbon.**

A soil that breathes harder is a soil that is coming back. That reading is the earliest honest signal Aura has.

---

### Why does biodiversity matter?

Because it is what does the work Aura would otherwise have to buy.

The estate is not a coffee field with trees on it. It is a four-story forest that produces coffee:
- **Emergent** — silver oak, fig, jackfruit. Temperature, carbon, hornbill nesting.
- **Canopy** — areca palm and native hardwoods. Mid-shade, and the trellis the pepper climbs.
- **Understory** — the coffee itself, reading whatever light falls through.
- **Ground** — pepper, cardamom, ginger, cover crop, and a litter layer treated as the skin of the ecosystem.

35,000 trees. Hornbills in the fig hollows. Macaques in the canopy. Fig wasps pollinating the keystone trees. Civets eating the pepper and the cherry. Bees marking the flowering seasons.

And the ecology is coupled — cut the top layer too hard and the areca palms stress; six to eight weeks later the pepper vines climbing them lose vigour. The intervention window is about three weeks. This is why Aura measures light before it cuts.

The forest is the farm.

---

### What role do cows play?

They are the engine. Not a detail, not a charming photograph — the source of almost everything the soil is fed.

Fifty-two Malnad Gidda, an indigenous Karnataka breed that has grazed these hills for centuries. Small-framed, hardy, adapted to this altitude and this monsoon. The breed is the point: their gut microbiome is shaped by these grasses and this soil, so their dung carries a microbial signature native to the ground it feeds. A commercial dairy breed cannot replicate it.

They graze the same 150 acres that grow the coffee, kept pesticide-free. What they take from a block returns to it as dung and urine. Nothing is bought in; nothing is trucked away.

Trace any preparation on the estate — the compost, the pit, the microbial brew, the sprays — back far enough and you arrive at these animals.

They are also cared for one at a time. Washed at dawn, dosed with plant-based sprays before anything synthetic, known by name. One of them was attacked by a leopard inside the farm, fought it off, and walked back alive.

---

### Why biodynamic?

Because it is the only agricultural tradition that treats the farm as one organism, and because the biology in it works.

Aura runs two systems at once. The European set — BD 500 through 508, the horn manure and horn silica and the six compost preparations. And the Vedic set, older and orally transmitted — Jeevamrit, Panchgavya, Beejamrit, Cow Pat Pit, Kunapjal from the Vrikshayurveda lineage.

Two languages describing the same observation: feed the soil, close the loop, let the cow do most of the work.

**And here is the sentence that makes this brand credible.** Aura has already published, on its own site, that the cosmology around these preparations — the lunar charts, the talk of the earth breathing — is poetry, and poetry has never survived a controlled trial. Aura keeps the preparations for a harder reason: the biology in them is real, measurable, and measured.

*The ritual earns its place by what it grows in the ground. That is the only test we let it take.*

Do not soften that. It is the single most persuasive paragraph in the entire body of material.

---

### Why does regeneration affect coffee quality?

Three mechanisms, all of them physical.

1. **Light.** Coffee under a managed canopy ripens slowly and evenly. Aura holds blocks to CCRI targets — 50,000–70,000 lux for Arabica — and the estate's own reading is that blocks at 65–75% canopy cover cup measurably better.
2. **Soil biology.** A living root zone with mycorrhizal reach feeds a cherry that accumulates more sugar. Brix across Aura's lots runs 18% to 23%.
3. **The ferment.** A healthy cherry arrives at the wet mill with its own microbial population intact — wild yeasts, lactic-acid bacteria — which is the raw material every ferment works with.

But Aura is careful here, and should stay careful: **altitude and variety set the ceiling. The ferment decides how much of that ceiling the cup reaches.** Anyone who says the process alone makes the coffee is skipping the harder half of the story.

---

### What does better farming mean for taste?

It means the cup stops being an accident.

Flavour is accumulated consequence. Every cup contains decisions made months earlier: canopy management, microbial health, rainfall timing, fermentation discipline, drying rhythm, and restraint. What ends up in the cup is what the land did, what the weather did, and what the team chose not to do.

The concrete version: **the same Sln.9, picked on the same morning from the same block, becomes six different coffees depending on what happens in the wet mill.** That is not terroir. That is terroir expressed through technique — and it is the most enjoyable proof of the whole thesis, because you can taste the difference in a single sitting.

---

### How is Aura different?

**One.** The loop is real and it is closed. Not "we compost". The estate's entire fertility programme — around 154,000 litres of Jeevamrit and 2,420 kg of CPP a year — is made from fifty-two cattle grazing the same ground as the coffee.

**Two.** Everything is measured, including the things most farms only feel. Light in lux before a branch comes down. pH every fifteen minutes through a ferment. Soil respiration monthly. Earthworms quarterly. Fourteen ecological programmes rolled into one Ecological Health Index per block.

**Three.** Nothing untested reaches the soil. Every preparation batch is read in the estate's own lab before and after. A failing batch is corrected or held. The block is retested ninety days on.

**Four, and most unusual:** Aura publishes what it does not know. The Solera carry-forward is an open question and Aura says so. That refusal to oversell is the brand.

---

### What can Aura prove?

Today, with records in hand:

- Six lots, individually documented — harvest weight, immature removed, floats removed, Brix, water TDS, ferment hours, drying days, drying method.
- Ferment logs — pH every 15 minutes, temperature three times daily, Brix at start, middle and end.
- Preparation batch tests — pH, EC, microbial colony counts, *Trichoderma* and *Pseudomonas* viability, before and after.
- Soil re-tests at 90 days against the batch that fed the block.
- The 2026 pre-monsoon illuminance survey — five clusters, ten readings per cluster, fifty observations per acre, across roughly 80 acres. Block 3 (Byton Patte) resolved into three zones: ~33,000, ~62,000 and ~82,000 lux.
- Per-animal records — health, milk and urine daily; dung logged per herd each morning.
- Screen grading and defect analysis per SCA protocol.
- A signed event record: *BD 501 applied to Block 07 at 06:14, waning moon, humidity 78%, by Raju, from dung batch G-03.*

### Why should a roaster care?

Because separation and documentation are the two things a green buyer cannot get from a co-operative lot.

Six micro-lots from one estate, one harvest — each with its own file, each behaving differently in the roaster. Minimum 95% ripeness at pick. All floats removed. Ferment stopped at pH 4.2, not by eye. The variation between lots is deliberate and repeatable, which means a roaster can build a menu from one origin.

And the record answers the question buyers are increasingly asked by their own customers, without a certification body in the middle.

---

### Why should a coffee drinker care?

They shouldn't have to. That is the point.

The coffee has to be worth drinking on its own terms first. The circle is what makes it *interesting* — the second thing you learn, not the first thing you are told. A person who wants to know can follow the QR all the way down to the block and the morning. A person who just wants a good cup can ignore every word of it.

That is what transparency means here: the page stands complete whether or not anyone opens it.

---

### Why should someone visit Aura?

Because nothing on a screen tastes like anything.

The estate is open. The lab and cupping room are being built for exactly this — buyers tasting the harvest where it grew. And there is a second reason, which is the honest one: after ten minutes on the ground the argument stops being an argument. You can smell the ferment shed. You can watch someone stir a barrel for forty-five minutes. You can stand under four stories of canopy and understand instantly why the coffee tastes the way it does.

*If the cup interests you, the estate is open.*

## CHAPTER 06 — WE MEASURE THE LIGHT BEFORE WE CUT

**Core thought:** Regenerative is not a feeling. It is a measurement taken before an irreversible decision.

**Hero line:**
> Measure first. Prune later. Validate afterwards.

**Supporting story:** Shade has been managed by eye for three centuries here — a contractor reads the canopy, judges what to take, directs the cut. Done by feel it opens the canopy unevenly and distorts a tree that will stand another fifty years.

In the 2026 pre-monsoon season Aura rebuilt the practice around a number. Nayana and Jagadeshwari walked the blocks with two lux meters. Five clusters per acre, ten readings each — fifty observations an acre. Each block resolved into zones: dense, medium, open. The cut answers the zone, not the block.

Then the wood stays where it fell, and every major cut is sealed with cow pat preparation and turmeric.

**Proof:**

- Block 3 (Byton Patte), 2026: Zone A ~33,000 · Zone B ~62,000 · Zone C ~82,000 lux

## CHAPTER 07 — SIX DECISIONS IN THE WET MILL

**Core thought:** The same cherry, six ways. This is where the story becomes delicious.

**Hero line:**
> The same cherry, picked the same morning, becomes six different coffees.

**Supporting story:** Only fully ripe cherries are plucked — minimum 95% ripeness. Every float is removed. Then the decisions begin, and they are decisions, not accidents: sealed or open, 24 hours or 48, banana leaves or none, mucilage on or washed away, thick drying then thin.

Underneath the romance is a microbial event with a known cast — wild yeasts and lactic-acid bacteria dissolving the mucilage while the pH falls. The alcohols and acids they leave behind become the esters a taster later calls fruity. The same microbes build faults if they run too long or too warm. Which is why every lot is held to a clock and a pH rather than left to chance.

The six lots, by name:
| Lot | Method | Signature |
|---|---|---|
| 001 | Anaerobic Natural | 48 hr sealed. The cherry's own microbes do the work. |
| 002 | Dry Osmosis | Dried to 45% moisture first. Blueberry, fig, raisin. |
| 003 | Red Honey — "Liquid Gold" | Mucilage on. 5 days thick drying, then 25 thin. Honey, caramel, nut. |
| 004 | Banana Wash | Layered under banana leaves for 48 hr. The most distinctly Indian lot. |
| 005 | Solera Maceration | 50% carry-forward into fresh cherry. The flagship. |
| 006 | Solera Wash | Brix 23%, the highest. Everything non-essential stripped away. |

**Proof:** Full lot files — harvest weight, immature removed, floats, Brix, water TDS, ferment hours, drying days. All on record.

**Required content:** The six lot cards. Ferment protocol: pH 4.2, pH read every 15 min, temperature 3×/day.
